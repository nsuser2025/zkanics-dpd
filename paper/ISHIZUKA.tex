\section{INTRODUCTION}
On a rainy day, raindrops on a car window merge together and race down the glass like shooting stars.
Such coalescence events are ubiquitous in nature and technology, and their outcomes
strongly depend not only on the characteristics of the underlying surface
but also on the physicochemical properties of the droplets.
Droplet coalescence on solid surfaces is a fundamental interfacial phenomenon in which the interplay
between fluid-fluid and fluid-solid interactions governs the resulting dynamics.
Particularly on superhydrophobic surfaces, the merged droplet can spontaneously
jump away from the surface following coalescence.
This phenomenon, commonly known as coalescence-induced droplet jumping (CIDJ),
has attracted considerable attention owing to its intriguing physics and potential
applications in wafer cleaning, watermark prevention, and electronic cooling
in semiconductor technologies.
In the absence of a solid wall, the coalescence of two identical droplets
generates a highly symmetric flow field.
The shape and motion of the merged droplet can therefore be described
by spatially symmetric functions.
The presence of a superhydrophobic surface, however, breaks this symmetry and
introduces a preferred direction normal to the surface.
Under suitable conditions, part of the excess surface energy released during coalescence
can then be converted into net translational motion, leading to droplet jumping.
What, then, governs droplet jumping when two non-identical droplets coalesce?

Continuum-based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods, such as the volume-of-fluid (VOF)
and level-set methods, have been widely used to simulate CIDJ.
Droplet jumping has been investigated in terms of the effects of fluid and interfacial properties,
including viscosity, droplet size, and surface tension.
These effects are commonly characterized by the dimensionless
Ohnesorge number, $Oh$.
In continuum descriptions, droplets are generally treated as homogeneous fluids,
making it difficult to explicitly account for microscopic heterogeneity
in their physicochemical properties.
Consequently, continuum-based methods are not well suited to capture
how microscopic heterogeneity and interparticle interactions, such as those arising
from differences in composition and miscibility between coalescing
droplets, affect the coalescence dynamics.

To address this issue, we employ many-body dissipative particle dynamics (MDPD),
a mesoscopic particle-based simulation technique
that provides a useful framework for describing fluid flows
while retaining explicit particle degrees of freedom.
In contrast to the standard dissipative particle dynamics (DPD),
the many-body interactions in MDPD generate a non-monotonic
equation of state (EoS) that exhibits a van der Waals loop
with a mechanically unstable region,
where $(\partial p/\partial \rho)_T < 0$.
This feature enables liquid–vapor coexistence
to emerge spontaneously within the simulation.
More importantly, the explicit particle representation of MDPD
allows the properties of the two coalescing droplets to be independently
controlled by varying the fluid particles' parameters.

In the present study, we investigate how differences in the properties
of the coalescing droplets affect CIDJ using MDPD simulations.
First, we validate MDPD as an alternative approach for simulating
CIDJ by comparing its results with those obtained from
VOF simulations of two identical droplets.
Subsequently, we investigate the effects of particle-mass heterogeneity on CIDJ.
Differences in particle mass do not affect the droplet size,
contact angle, or surface energy, but they break the symmetry
of the kinetic moments after coalescence.
Furthermore, we investigate how asymmetry in surface affinity between
the two coalescing droplets affects the resulting jumping dynamics
by varying the interaction between fluid and solid particles.
Because miscibility represents an important source of chemical asymmetry in CIDJ,
we systematically vary the degree of miscibility between the two coalescing droplets
by tuning their inter-species interactions.
We additionally consider the coalescence of multicomponent
droplets to examine the effects of compositional heterogeneity on CIDJ.
